Chapter 2 • Data Transmission

Data Transmission

Follow the data from sender to receiver.

Full interactive notes built around the Cambridge syllabus and the supplied question-paper/mark-scheme patterns: packet switching, transmission methods, error detection, ARQ and encryption.

1

Packets & Packet Structure

Large transmissions are broken into manageable units.

Before transmission, data is broken down into packets. A packet has three main parts:

HEADER
PAYLOAD
TRAILER

Header

Control information needed to deliver and rebuild the transmission. The syllabus specifically requires:

  • destination address
  • originator's/source address
  • packet number

Payload

The actual data being transmitted.

Trailer

Information placed at the end of the packet; keep the distinction between trailer and header clear.

Common confusion

The packet number is not the payload. It is header information that helps the receiver put packets back into the correct order.

MS depth: packet header

The header can contain the destination address/IP address, sender/originator address/IP address and packet number. The payload is the actual data being transmitted; the trailer contains end/error-checking information where applicable.

2

Packet Switching

Packets can travel independently and be reconstructed at the destination.

Process chain
data split into packets→router chooses route→packets may take different routes→may arrive out of order→reordered after final packet arrives

Describe packet switching as an ordered process. Avoid giving disconnected definitions of “packet” and “router.”

MS depth: router and reassembly

A router can control the route a packet takes, send each packet towards its destination and choose a more efficient route. Packets from one message can take different routes, can arrive out of order, and packet numbers allow them to be put back into the correct order at the destination.

3

Serial vs Parallel Transmission

How many bits are sent at the same time?

Serial

Data is sent one bit at a time along a single wire/channel.

Advantages: less interference/crosstalk, reliable over longer distances, fewer wires, often cheaper.

Disadvantage: fewer bits are sent simultaneously.

Parallel

Multiple bits are sent simultaneously using multiple wires/channels.

Advantages: can transfer several bits at once; suitable over short distances.

Disadvantages: more wires, more costly, risk of skew/interference/crosstalk over distance.

Scenario answer formula
method → technical property → consequence → why suitable here

For a printer on the same desk, do not stop at “parallel is faster” or “serial is cheaper.” Tie the property to the short distance, required speed, interference risk and cabling.

Serial/parallel describes how bits travel. It does not tell you whether communication is one-way or two-way. That is simplex/duplex.

MS comparison depth

Serial: one bit at a time down a single wire; generally cheaper cabling, less skew/crosstalk and more reliable over longer distances, but can be slower than parallel. Parallel: multiple bits are sent simultaneously using multiple wires/channels; it can transfer more bits at once but needs more wires and is more vulnerable to skew/crosstalk over distance. In scenario questions, connect the technical property to distance, required speed, cost and reliability.

4

Simplex, Half-Duplex & Full-Duplex

Which direction can the data travel, and when?

MethodDirectionMeaningThink of
SimplexA → BData travels in one direction only.One-way data flow
Half-duplexA ⇄ BBoth directions are possible, but not simultaneously.Take turns
Full-duplexA ⇄ BBoth directions can transmit at the same time.Simultaneous two-way

A supplied 2025 mark scheme demonstrates the precision expected for serial simplex: one bit at a time, down a single wire, and data only in one direction. These are separate creditworthy ideas.

Cambridge can combine dimensions: serial full-duplex, for example, tells you both how bits are sent and the permitted direction/timing of communication.

Combining transmission dimensions

Cambridge may combine the two classifications. For example, serial full-duplex means bits are sent one at a time while data can travel in both directions simultaneously; parallel half-duplex means multiple bits travel together and communication can go both ways, but not at the same time.

5

Universal Serial Bus (USB)

A standard interface for connecting peripherals and transmitting serial data.

How it is used

A USB interface provides a standard connection between devices. Data is transmitted serially through the USB connection. The interface can be used to connect peripherals and may also provide electrical power to a connected device.

Benefits & drawbacks

Benefits: widely supported standard, simple connection, plug-and-play/hot-swappable in typical systems, data and power can share the connection.

Drawbacks: connector/version differences can create compatibility issues; cable length and transmission performance are limited by the implementation/version.

The syllabus specifically requires benefits and drawbacks, so learn USB as more than the expansion of the acronym.

2025 MS: USB details

USB sends data one bit at a time down a single wire. Supplied mark schemes credit benefits including: the peripheral can be powered by the device; USB is a universal connection; it is backward compatible; there is less chance of skew/errors; different/adequate transmission speeds are supported; the cable cannot be inserted incorrectly; and the device can be automatically detected / its driver automatically installed. Drawbacks credited include slower transmission than a parallel connection and a wire that can get in the way or limit movement.

6

Why Transmission Errors Occur

Correct data can become incorrect while travelling.

Interference during transmission can cause data to be:

lost•gained/added•changed

Past mark schemes also recognise contexts such as crosstalk, collisions and other transmission/network problems.

Strong 2-mark chain: interference/crosstalk occurs during transmission → this causes data/bits to be lost, gained or changed.

Error detection does not automatically mean the method corrects the corrupted bit. Many methods establish that an error exists; retransmission may then be required.

Error cause → effect

Interference or crosstalk during transmission can cause data/bits to be lost, gained/added or changed. Keep the cause and its effect linked when the command is explain.

7

Parity Check

Add a parity bit so the number of 1s follows an agreed rule.

Even parity

The total number of 1s, including the parity bit, must be even.

Odd parity

The total number of 1s, including the parity bit, must be odd.

Interactive parity lab

Enter seven data bits and choose the parity rule. The tool calculates the eighth parity bit.

Detection process
  1. Sender counts the 1s in each byte.
  2. A parity bit is set so the byte matches the agreed odd/even parity.
  3. Byte is transmitted.
  4. Receiver counts the 1s again.
  5. If the received parity does not match the agreed rule, an error is detected.

Parity block / parity byte

Parity can be extended across a block of bytes. A parity byte provides parity information across corresponding bit positions, allowing checking in another dimension. You must be able to complete parity-block tables, not merely define parity.

A basic parity check may fail to detect some patterns of multiple-bit errors. Its job in this syllabus is to detect transmission errors, not identify the original value of every corrupted bit.

Parity at byte and block level

For parity, sender and receiver agree on odd or even parity. A parity bit is set so the total number of 1s has the required parity. The receiver counts/checks the 1s; the wrong parity indicates an error. With block parity, parity can be checked across rows/columns of a block to help identify the location of an erroneous bit.

8

Checksum

A calculated value travels with the data and is independently recalculated.

Mark-scheme process chain
  1. A checksum is calculated from the original data.
  2. A defined algorithm/calculation is used.
  3. The checksum is transmitted with the data.
  4. The receiving device recalculates the checksum from the received data.
  5. The received/recalculated values are compared.
  6. If they do not match, an error is indicated and retransmission can be requested.

For a multi-mark describe question, write this as an ordered process. “A checksum checks whether data is correct” is far too vague.

Checksum process

A checksum value is calculated from the data using an algorithm before transmission and sent with the data. The receiving device performs the corresponding calculation again and compares the result with the received checksum. A mismatch indicates that an error has occurred.

9

Echo Check

The receiver sends a copy back to the sender.

  1. Sender transmits the data.
  2. Receiver sends a copy of the received data back.
  3. Sender compares the returned copy with the original.
  4. If they differ, an error occurred and the data can be resent.

Do not confuse echo check with checksum. Echo check returns data; checksum recalculates a value.

Echo-check process

The receiver sends a copy of the received data back to the sender. The returned copy is compared with the original data. If the two do not match, an error is detected and the data can be sent again.

10

Check Digit

Primarily used to detect errors in data entry.

  1. A check digit is calculated from the other digits using an algorithm.
  2. The calculated digit is appended to/stored with the data.
  3. When the code is entered, the calculation is performed again.
  4. The result is compared with the entered/stored check digit.
  5. A mismatch indicates an entry error.

Required examples

ISBN and bar codes are explicitly named by the syllabus.

Key distinction

Check digit → data entry errors.
Parity/checksum/echo → typically transmission errors in this chapter.

Check digit process

A check digit is calculated from the other digits using an algorithm and appended to the data, for example in a barcode/ISBN. On data entry, the check digit is recalculated/checked using the entered digits. If the calculated value does not match the entered check digit, a data-entry error is detected.

11

Automatic Repeat Query (ARQ)

Acknowledgement + timeout + retransmission.

send packet→start timer→receiver checks data→acknowledgement→next packet

If a valid acknowledgement is not received before the timeout, the packet is resent.

Positive acknowledgement

Indicates successful/error-free receipt so transmission can continue.

Negative acknowledgement

Indicates a problem and can trigger retransmission. The syllabus requires awareness of positive/negative acknowledgements and timeout.

A strong ARQ description explicitly names acknowledgement and timeout. A 2024 supplied MS combines parity checking with ARQ in an 8-mark process question, showing that Cambridge can connect error detection and retransmission rather than testing each idea in isolation.

ARQ — full process

Automatic Repeat reQuest (ARQ) uses an acknowledgement and a timeout. After data is sent, a timer starts. If the receiver confirms valid data, an acknowledgement can be returned and the sender continues. If the data is invalid, a negative acknowledgement may be returned; alternatively an expected positive acknowledgement may not arrive. If the required acknowledgement is not received before timeout, the data/packet is retransmitted.

Parity + ARQ together

Supplied papers combine methods: receiver checks the agreed parity → valid parity can lead to a positive acknowledgement → invalid parity means no positive acknowledgement / may produce a negative acknowledgement → timeout or NAK causes retransmission.

12

Encryption

Protect transmitted data from being understood if intercepted.

plaintext→encryption algorithm + key→ciphertext→decryption→plaintext

Plaintext is readable original data. Ciphertext is encrypted data that should be meaningless without the appropriate key.

Purpose

Encryption protects the confidentiality of transmitted data. If encrypted data is intercepted, the interceptor should not be able to understand it without the required key. Encryption does not by itself prevent interception.

Symmetric encryption

The same shared secret key is used for encryption and decryption. The key therefore needs to be known securely by the communicating parties.

Asymmetric encryption

Uses a mathematically related public key and private key. The syllabus explicitly requires the public/private-key idea.

For confidentiality, data encrypted using the recipient's public key can be decrypted using the corresponding private key.

Terminology bank
plaintext ciphertext algorithm key public key private key symmetric asymmetric

Wrong idea: “Encryption stops hackers from getting the data.” Better: encrypted data may still be intercepted, but it is intended to be unreadable without the appropriate key.

Encryption — exact comparison depth

The purpose of encryption is that if transmitted data is intercepted, it cannot be understood without the required key; encryption does not prevent interception itself. Symmetric encryption uses a shared key for both encryption and decryption, so sender and receiver know the same key. Asymmetric encryption uses different keys: a public key can be known by anyone and is used to encrypt, while the corresponding private key is kept by the intended recipient and is used to decrypt.
